Why there's a v2

I gave this talk. Then I
went and did the work.

Version 1 ended on: "the future isn't AI vs. humans — it's humans who use AI vs. humans who don't."

— me, being applauded for saying nothing

It's true. It's also unfalsifiable, unactionable, and agreed with by everyone in the room. That's how you know it's not a finding.

v1 is still up, unedited — /v1. Deleting it would prove the point wrong.

The claim

Agents didn't remove the work.
They relocated it.

Production collapsed to near-zero

Drafts, specs, prototypes, research, refactors. The thing we spent a decade optimising is now the cheap part.

Verification did not

Knowing whether the output is right costs exactly what it always did. Sometimes more, because it now arrives fluent.

Every hard thing in this talk falls out of that one asymmetry.

Part 1

Six Field Notes

Things that actually happened. Including the ones where I was wrong.

Note 01

The plan was already done

I asked a frontier model for a strategy to fix how I show up in AI answers. It produced a confident, well-structured, three-phase plan.

~80%

of it was already shipped. Months earlier. It had no way to check — so it didn't.

The note: agents produce plausible plans, not true ones. It looked like strategy. It was a template with my name in it.

Note 02

It was right about the facts
and wrong about the question

What it did well

Researched it properly. Found four established owners of the territory. Advised me not to build it. All correct.

then

What flipped it

I mentioned a phrase I'd used for months. The recommendation inverted completely — in one turn.

It was answering "is this observation novel?" — when the question that mattered was "is this name available?"

The note: it had the better research. I had the tacit knowledge that made the research relevant.

Note 03

The failure mode isn't refusal.
It's fluent wrongness.

I searched for a term I'd coined. I got back a detailed, well-cited, internally coherent answer about a real arXiv paper on training collapse in reinforcement learning.

Entirely unrelated to what I meant. Nothing in the answer signalled that.

The note: models rarely fail by declining. They fail by answering. Fluency is not evidence, and it actively conceals the gap where evidence should be.

Note 04

The bug nobody could see

One page on my site had been quietly telling Google "I'm a duplicate of the homepage — don't index me." Live for months. Invisible unless you view source on exactly the right page.

Asking for an opinion

"Review my site for SEO issues" → a tidy, generic list. Missed it entirely.

Asking for a harness

A script walking all 32 sitemap URLs, comparing each to its canonical tag. 32 URLs, 1 problem. Seconds.

The note: agents are mediocre at noticing and excellent at checking. Don't ask what they think. Ask them to build the thing that would catch it.

Note 05

The most valuable output was
"I can't verify this"

I said a talk of mine was from a major conference. The agent checked: the recording carried no branding from that event, and it published three months afterwards.

It flagged the discrepancy instead of publishing the claim. On my own CV.

The note: a system that surfaces its uncertainty beats one that's right slightly more often. When you're the accountable one, calibration is worth more than accuracy.

The one idea worth stealing

The Verification Ceiling

You can safely delegate exactly as much as you can check.
Past that line you are not managing. You are hoping.

Raise the ceiling

Evals, harnesses, tests, checkable acceptance criteria. Core PM work now — not an engineering chore.

Or lower the delegation

Hand over less until you can check more. Unglamorous. Correct.

Most teams do neither, and call the gap velocity.

A word I'd retire

"Orchestration" makes it sound like
a scheduling problem

v1 of this talk leaned on it heavily. It implies the hard part is coordinating the agents — who runs what, in what order.

In practice, coordination was the easy part. The hard part was judgment, applied to output that arrives sounding certain.

A conductor whose orchestra plays confidently out of tune doesn't need better scheduling. They need an ear.

The scarce input

Taste stopped being
a soft skill

Knowing what's good

When ten plausible options cost nothing, choosing is the whole value-add.

Knowing what's missing

Models can't tell you about the constraint nobody wrote down. You can.

Knowing when to stop

Infinite cheap iteration has no natural stopping point. That has to come from you.

None of this is new to PM. It's just that everything else got automated out from under it.

Part 3

What I'd Actually Do

Four practices. All of them boring. All of them load-bearing.

Practices

Four things that actually worked

01 — Ask for the harness, not the opinion

"Review this" gets you a tidy list. "Write the check that would catch this" gets you the bug.

02 — Supply the tacit knowledge early

The thing you know and never wrote down is the only input the model can't get elsewhere. Lead with it.

03 — Make "I don't know" safe

Explicitly ask what it couldn't verify. Reward the flag. An unflagged guess is the expensive failure.

04 — Never ship what you can't defend

If you can't explain why, in your own words, without the transcript — you haven't decided. You've forwarded.

Takeaways

Production is free.
Judgment isn't.

The constraint moved. Most orgs are still optimising the half that stopped being the bottleneck.

Delegate to your ceiling.

You can hand over exactly as much as you can check. Build checks or hand over less.

Fluent ≠ correct.

The dangerous output isn't the refusal. It's the confident, well-cited, plausible answer to the wrong question.

AI didn't make the job easier.

It made the easy parts free — and left me
with only the hard parts.

That's a better job. It is not a lighter one.
