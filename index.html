Why there's a v2

I gave this talk. Then I
went and did the work.

Version 1 ended on: "the future isn't AI vs. humans — it's humans who use AI vs. humans who don't."

— me, being applauded for saying nothing

It's true. It's also unfalsifiable, unactionable, and agreed with by everyone in the room. That's how you know it's not a finding.

v1 is still up, unedited — /v1. Deleting it would prove the point wrong.

The claim

Agents didn't remove the work.
They relocated it.

Production collapsed to near-zero

Drafts, specs, prototypes, research, refactors. The thing we spent a decade optimising is now the cheap part.

Verification did not

Knowing whether the output is right costs exactly what it always did. Sometimes more, because it now arrives fluent.

Every hard thing in this talk falls out of that one asymmetry.

Part 1

Six Field Notes

A year of shipping AI security product with agents in the loop.
Including the parts where I was the problem.

Note 01

Seven versions.
Seventeen days.

One spec. v1.7 through v3.0 in under three weeks — markdown, Word and PowerPoint regenerated in lockstep every time. Fourteen of those commits contain no new thinking at all. They just rebuild the deliverables.

Producing a version cost me minutes. Agreeing on one took the seventeen days.

The note: when regeneration is free, the document stops being the bottleneck and the alignment underneath it becomes the whole job. I didn't write a spec seven times. I ran seven rounds of negotiation and let the spec keep up.

Note 02

One column name.
Five spellings. Fourteen days.

A single field in a schema proposal changed name five times. Each rename propagated instantly across specs, queries, a Word doc and a deck — dozens of files, seconds, zero errors. Genuinely superhuman.

It could not tell me which name was right even once.

That came from a reviewer's convention, a naming standard nobody had written down, and a judgment call about what a future reader would assume. None of it was in the repo.

The note: agents made renaming free. They did not make naming easier. Naming was always the job.

Note 03

A PM's repo is not
a code repo

One year of my actual work, by commit type:

86

documents

30

features built

22

corrections

The note: the artifact of product management is prose — and prose is exactly what these models are best at. That's why the leverage is enormous, and why the failure mode is invisible. Bad code crashes. A bad paragraph gets approved.

Note 04

It wrote the most satisfying version
of the market, not the truest

A competitive landscape refresh across 26 vendor files. What came back was a genuinely compelling narrative — clean arc, causal, quotable.

What I had to commit

"Remove speculative narrative — let facts lead." Then: verify every executive's current role. One was simply out of date.

What fixed it

An evidence-based maturity rating on every claim. If it isn't in a published source, it doesn't get a number.

The note: "compelling" is a failure mode when you're briefing leadership. The story was better than the evidence, and I'm the one whose name was on the cover.

Note 05

It handed me my own
misconception, beautifully formatted

I had two internal platforms confused — genuinely, in my own head. I briefed the agent from that confusion.

It produced a clear, well-structured, internally consistent document built entirely on the error — and cited my own earlier notes as the source.

The commit that fixed it is one line. Finding it took a conversation with someone who actually owned the platform.

The note: agents are mirrors with excellent grammar. Your wrong model of the world comes back laundered as an authoritative document with footnotes. Garbage in → credible garbage out.

Note 06

It answered all 23 comments.
It couldn't read the room.

A spec review came back with 23 comments. The agent drafted a reply to every one in a single pass — structurally sound, correctly scoped, politely worded.

Free

Drafting 23 coherent responses. Threading them back into the document. Rebuilding every deliverable.

Entirely mine

Which to accept, which to push back on, and the two that weren't technical comments at all.

The note: a comment is rarely just its text. Some are questions, some are positions, and some are someone telling you they weren't consulted. Only one of those is answerable in a document.

Part 2

The System I Actually Run

Because "use AI more" is not an operating model.

The setup

One repo. It contains
almost no code.

Specs, competitive research, meeting notes, feature docs, queries, drafts — and the configuration that lets agents work on all of it. It is a PM operating system kept in version control.

Conventions file

How work is structured, where things live, what "done" means. Read by every agent, every session.

Cross-session context file

Active projects, decisions, standing preferences, hard-won learnings. Memory as a file I can edit — not a feature I have to trust.

Version-controlled context is the single highest-leverage thing I did. Everything below depends on it.

The roster

Ten narrow agents,
not one clever one

Feature co-pilot

Status, decisions, action items per feature

Compete research

Vendor landscape, refreshed on a cadence

Spec writer

Structured docs in a fixed house format

Meeting prep

Briefs pulled from real prior context

Weekly recap

What actually moved, not what was busy

Feedback analyzer

Customer notes → themes, with counts

Plus email, content, profile and UX review. A router maps intent → agent, so I never pick from a menu.

The reason to keep them narrow: a narrow agent can be evaluated. A general one can only be liked.

The four that mattered

What actually moved
the quality needle

01 — Config is the source of truth

To change what an agent tracks, edit the config file — never re-explain yourself in chat. Chat doesn't persist. Files do.

02 — Progressive disclosure

Don't front-load everything into the prompt. Point at files and let the agent go get what it needs. Smaller instructions, better results.

03 — Three-tier boundaries

✅ Always  /  ⚠️ Ask first  /  🚫 Never. The middle tier is the one that earns its keep — and the one most people skip.

04 — Calibrate on real artifacts

First drafts are structurally right and tonally dead. Feed it things you actually sent, and the voice problem disappears.

Non-negotiable

Two rules I'd never drop

1. Treat every output as if it will be forwarded.

Because eventually one is. Write for the reader you didn't plan for.

2. Never put a credential in a conversation.

Chat history isn't a whiteboard. You cannot un-say it, and you usually can't delete it either.

I work in AI security, so I'll say the quiet part: the context window is an attack surface, and everything you paste into it is now part of your threat model.

The unglamorous half

Half of getting good at this
was deleting things

Custom scrapers, bespoke integrations, hand-built tools, whole apps. Most were genuinely useful when I built them. Most were dead weight within two months.

Not because they were badly made — because the models improved past the need for them.

This repo was literally called vibecoding before it became a command center. That rename is the honest summary of the year.

The note: re-examine your scaffolding every few months. Tooling that once added capability starts quietly constraining it. Budget for demolition.

What It Means

If production is free, what exactly is the job?

The new economics

What got cheap.
What didn't.

Approaching free

  • First drafts of anything
  • Research summaries
  • Prototypes & refactors
  • Generating options
vs

Unchanged or worse

  • Knowing which option
  • Catching plausible-but-wrong
  • Context nobody wrote down
  • Being accountable for it

Note that the right-hand column is the entire job description.

The one idea worth stealing

The Verification Ceiling

You can safely delegate exactly as much as you can check.
Past that line you are not managing. You are hoping.

Raise the ceiling

Evals, harnesses, tests, checkable acceptance criteria. Core PM work now — not an engineering chore.

Or lower the delegation

Hand over less until you can check more. Unglamorous. Correct.

Most teams do neither, and call the gap velocity.

A word I'd retire

"Orchestration" makes it sound like
a scheduling problem

v1 of this talk leaned on it heavily. It implies the hard part is coordinating the agents — who runs what, in what order.

In practice, coordination was the easy part. The hard part was judgment, applied to output that arrives sounding certain.

A conductor whose orchestra plays confidently out of tune doesn't need better scheduling. They need an ear.

The scarce input

Taste stopped being
a soft skill

Knowing what's good

When ten plausible options cost nothing, choosing is the whole value-add.

Knowing what's missing

Models can't tell you about the constraint nobody wrote down. You can.

Knowing when to stop

Infinite cheap iteration has no natural stopping point. That has to come from you.

None of this is new to PM. It's just that everything else got automated out from under it.

Part 4

What I'd Actually Do

Four practices. All of them boring. All of them load-bearing.

Practices

Four things that actually worked

01 — Put the context in a file, not a chat

Anything you explain twice belongs in a conventions file. Chat is a session; files are an asset.

02 — Supply the tacit knowledge early

The convention nobody wrote down is the only input the model can't get elsewhere. Lead with it, or fix it five renames later.

03 — Make "I don't know" safe

Ask explicitly what it couldn't verify, and reward the flag. An unflagged guess in a leadership doc is the expensive failure.

04 — Never ship what you can't defend

If you can't explain why, in your own words, without the transcript — you haven't decided. You've forwarded.

Takeaways

Production is free.
Judgment isn't.

The constraint moved. Most orgs are still optimising the half that stopped being the bottleneck.

Delegate to your ceiling.

You can hand over exactly as much as you can check. Build checks or hand over less.

Fluent ≠ correct.

The dangerous output isn't the refusal. It's the confident, well-cited, plausible answer to the wrong question.

AI didn't make the job easier.

It made the easy parts free — and left me
with only the hard parts.

That's a better job. It is not a lighter one.
